Architecture context in a composable enterprise

Part 3 of 5

Preservation is not keeping the old platform alive

When an architecture repository reaches the end of its operational life, organizations often face an uncomfortable choice. Keep the old platform available for reference, with its licences and maintenance burden, or export files that few people can meaningfully inspect later.

That is a false choice.

Repository preservation should not mean running a retired product indefinitely. It should mean retaining the useful knowledge, structure and evidence in a form that remains accessible after the original application has gone.

An export is a source, not an archive box

A one-time export is often treated as the final administrative step in a migration. The files are stored, the retention requirement is satisfied and the programme moves on. But formal retention and practical accessibility are different outcomes.

Retention answers whether the information still exists. Preservation answers whether it can still be understood and used.

If the export contains objects, relationships and diagrams, those structures can remain valuable. They explain why an earlier architecture looked the way it did, support audits and help teams understand dependencies that have not disappeared merely because the modelling tool changed.

In an anonymized repository-preservation engagement, a large construction organization needed this knowledge to remain available for a defined period after its previous repository was retired. The old platform was not kept running. The export was treated as a governed source and made available through a new experience in which authorized users could inspect objects and relationships, navigate diagrams, search and report.

The distinction is precise: the delivery did not emulate the retired repository. It made the preserved knowledge usable. This is the kind of problem ARC Access is intended to address.

Preservation needs to be designed, not merely stored

An archive is usually designed for custody. A preservation service must also be designed for retrieval and interpretation.

That requires decisions about structure, identifiers, provenance, access, reporting and the expected life of the preserved material. It also requires restraint. Preservation should not silently transform historical evidence into a new asserted current state.

The source must remain identifiable as a snapshot. Users should understand when it was captured and what it can legitimately support. If later sources are connected, historical and current information must remain distinguishable.

Time is part of provenance: knowing where information came from is not enough if we no longer know when it described the enterprise.

Decommission the platform without discarding the context

This approach changes the economics of repository retirement. The organization can remove the operational dependency on the old product while retaining access to knowledge that still has legal, architectural or practical value.

It also creates a better foundation for future modernization. Preserved knowledge can be connected selectively when a new decision requires it. Teams do not need to migrate everything into a replacement repository merely to avoid losing access.

For architecture leaders, the useful question is therefore not “how long must we keep this platform alive?” It is “which knowledge must remain intelligible, to whom, and for what decisions?”

Once that is clear, preservation becomes an intentional architecture capability rather than the final unresolved task in a migration plan.

The platform can retire. The context does not have to disappear with it.
