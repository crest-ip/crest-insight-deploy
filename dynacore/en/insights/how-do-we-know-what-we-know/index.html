Architecture context in a composable enterprise

Part 5 of 5

How do we know what we know?

Architecture environments contain statements with very different foundations. One may come directly from an authoritative operational source. Another may be inferred by relating several records. A third may be a proposed future state awaiting review.

They may describe the same part of the enterprise, but they do not carry the same status.

This creates a question that becomes more important as architecture work becomes increasingly assisted: how do we know what we know?

Generated does not mean established

A system can extract a candidate application, capability or relationship from a document. That does not make the result part of the approved architecture.

The proposal needs a visible source. The extraction needs a confidence or review state. An architect needs to distinguish what was observed, what was inferred, what has merely been proposed and what has been accepted. If the source changes, the organization needs to understand what conclusions may be affected.

These controls are especially important when several sources disagree. Automation should expose the conflict and prepare it for resolution, not silently select the most convenient answer.

Human review is therefore not a temporary concession until the model improves. It is part of the operating design for accountable architecture.

Knowledge needs a state, not only a location

An active architecture environment therefore needs more than content and relationships. It needs explicit knowledge states.

  • Observed knowledge comes directly from an identified source.
  • Inferred knowledge has been derived from one or more observations.
  • Proposed knowledge is a candidate for the architecture or a possible future state.
  • Reviewed knowledge has been assessed by an accountable person.
  • Accepted knowledge has been approved as part of the architecture context used for action.

These states do not need to form one irreversible sequence in every workflow. Their purpose is to prevent a generated suggestion, a source observation and an approved decision from becoming indistinguishable simply because they are stored together.

Preserving those distinctions allows automation to assist reasoning without silently rewriting reality.

Trust is an architectural output

Automation is often justified through speed. In enterprise architecture, trust is equally important.

A slower manual process can still be unsafe if its assumptions are invisible. A faster assisted process can be responsible if it preserves evidence, access rules, review and a clear record of why an action was accepted.

This gives Chief Architects a practical test for every automation proposal:

  • Can we trace the proposed conclusion to its sources?
  • Can authorized reviewers inspect the relevant context?
  • Can we distinguish inference from confirmed knowledge?
  • Can a person stop, correct or reject the next action?
  • Will the decision remain intelligible after the workflow has completed?

A “no” to any of these questions identifies the control that must be strengthened before that step should be automated. The remedy is not necessarily a better model. It may be better access, clearer provenance or a more explicit review boundary.

Trust is not the absence of uncertainty. It is the ability to see what is known, why it is believed, what remains unresolved and who has accepted responsibility for the next action.

That is what turns connected information into trustworthy architectural context.
