What are we actually getting for our money?

Part 4 of 5

We needed an answer by Friday

The question arrived on Tuesday.

The conditions for a planned change had shifted. By Friday, management needed to know whether the timetable was still realistic and which risks a decision to proceed would entail.

We had three working days.

There was no opportunity to conduct a complete analysis. Nor could we answer on instinct alone.

We began with the decision

The team clarified what management actually needed to decide.

Which parts of the organisation might be affected? Which dependencies could change the timetable? Which uncertainties were significant enough to require attention before the decision was made?

We then used our AI agents to examine the business knowledge we had already built and compare it with the new material.

We used a shared ontology to distinguish business capabilities, processes, information objects, applications and their relationships. But our knowledge of the organisation itself came from existing models, documented relationships, sources and earlier judgements.

It was important to keep the two apart.

The ontology gave us a shared structure. The business model and its sources gave us the evidence for the analysis.

The results arrived quickly. They also contained contradictions and gaps.

A critical integration was documented, but its owner was unclear. An information need appeared in several processes but lacked a shared definition. A possible dependency between two applications had been identified, but was supported only by old project material.

We could have hidden these uncertainties and presented a clearer answer. We could also have requested more time and left management without useful evidence.

Neither option was good enough.

Reliable enough is an active decision

We divided the analysis into three parts:

  • verified conditions and consequences clearly supported by the evidence;
  • professional judgements with visible assumptions; and
  • open questions that could affect the recommendation.

For every open question, we identified who needed to contribute an answer and when that answer could be available.

Two questions had to be resolved before the decision. Three could be handled as explicit conditions during implementation. We judged the remaining uncertainties not to be decisive for this particular decision.

That did not make them unimportant. It meant that they did not need to be resolved now.

It was my responsibility to stand behind that boundary and make it clear to management.

AI could help us identify relationships, compare evidence and flag possible discrepancies. The architects could assess quality in their respective fields.

But someone had to take responsibility for which conclusions the team could support, which uncertainties had to be raised, and what we would not have time to investigate.

I could not delegate that responsibility to an agent.

Management did not receive a perfect answer

It received a useful one.

Our recommendation was to proceed, subject to two explicit conditions and a changed sequence of implementation.

Management could see which sources supported the conclusion, where the team had made professional judgements and which risks remained. It could also see which questions required follow-up and who was responsible for resolving them.

The answer was not less professional because every issue had not been fully investigated. It was more professional than a confident recommendation that concealed uncertainty.

I thought back to the management meeting a few months earlier, when I had tried to describe our value by reporting how much we had produced.

This time, I did not need to explain how many models we had updated or how many analyses our agents had performed.

I could show which decision we had helped the organisation make — and which risks we had made visible before it did so.

That was a far better account of our work.

The value of architecture that week was not the number of models we managed to update. It was that the organisation could make a time-critical decision without confusing speed with certainty.
