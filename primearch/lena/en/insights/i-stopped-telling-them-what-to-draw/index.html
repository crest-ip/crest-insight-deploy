What are we actually getting for our money?

Part 3 of 5

I stopped telling them what to draw

Our backlog was full of requests for models.

Capability map. Process model. Information model. Application landscape.

It looked structured.

Yet our prioritisation meetings often ended with the same question: where should we begin?

I realised that we were directing the team through formats rather than through the needs of the organisation.

So I stopped beginning with what they should draw.

The question came first

Instead, one assignment was framed like this:

“Management needs to decide whether the change can be completed before the next budget period. Which business capabilities, information needs and application dependencies could affect that decision?”

Another began with:

“What risks arise if this service is moved before we have resolved how the shared information should be handled?”

They were still architecture questions. But they started with something the organisation needed to understand or decide.

The team determined which views and analyses were required. Sometimes that meant a process model. Sometimes a capability map or an information model. Sometimes a simple account of dependencies and open questions was enough.

That gave the team more freedom, not less.

At the same time, we established shared quality criteria.

We had to be able to explain which business objects and relationships supported a conclusion. Important claims had to be traceable to sources or clearly identified as professional judgements. Uncertainty had to remain visible.

And every delivery had to state which decision it could support — and which decision it could not yet support.

A foundation for people and agents

We began using Prime Arch as a shared ontology for describing the organisation consistently.

It gave us defined types of business objects and relationships, together with guidance on how they could be used.

That did not mean Prime Arch contained our organisation’s actual processes, capabilities, information objects or applications. We still had to build and govern that knowledge ourselves.

We also had to make it available to the agents supporting our work.

This did not require everyone to use the same view or tool. Experienced specialists could continue approaching problems from different directions, and their agents could analyse different kinds of material.

But when the same business object appeared in several analyses, we needed to be able to reuse its identity and established relationships.

If an agent proposed a new object, we had to determine whether it really was new or had already been described. If it proposed a relationship, we had to distinguish between the relationship type being meaningful according to the ontology and the actual relationship being supported by evidence from the organisation.

Those are two different quality questions. Both must be handled.

Differences between analyses did not disappear automatically, but they became easier to identify and understand.

That is an important distinction for a leader.

Professional disagreement can create value when we know precisely what we disagree about. Accidental shifts in meaning mostly create rework.

I also had to start saying no

Once the connection to the business question became clearer, we discovered how many assignments had no real recipient.

Some were removed. Others were narrowed. A few had to wait until someone could explain which decision the result was meant to support.

That was not popular everywhere.

Previously, I had tried to be accommodating and find room for as many assignments as possible. It felt like a way to demonstrate the relevance of the architecture team.

But a full backlog is not the same as valuable work. Saying yes to everything is not the same as taking responsibility for the whole.

Leadership is not about creating complete agreement around the backlog. It is about taking responsibility for direction when demand exceeds capacity.

I had not stopped directing the team.

I had moved that direction away from the pictures they should produce and towards the questions we needed to answer together.

That shift was about to become crucial when we received a question that could not wait.
