What are we actually getting for our money?

Part 2 of 5

Three architects, three AI agents, three realities

I had encouraged the team to experiment with AI.

One architect used an agent to analyse business capabilities. Another described processes and information needs. A third mapped applications and integrations.

The results were impressive. In a few days, they produced material that would previously have taken weeks.

Then we tried to combine it.

The same business area had three names. A capability in one analysis had become a process in another, without anyone explaining the difference. Information objects that looked identical had different boundaries. An integration was verified in one source and assumed in another.

Three capable people. Three capable agents. Three descriptions of the same organisation.

It was not an individual quality problem

Each analysis could be defended on its own terms. The people had worked professionally, and their agents had received relevant material and reasonable instructions.

The problem existed between the deliverables.

We had increased individual productivity without creating the conditions for the results to become shared knowledge. As production accelerated, so did the rate at which differences were embedded.

My first instinct was to gather the team and review each model. We could have done that, but we would only have corrected the symptoms. The next agent or analysis could recreate the same problem.

This could not be solved by asking everyone to write a better prompt. It was a leadership problem.

Independence requires something shared

I do not want to prescribe how experienced architects solve their assignments. Their different perspectives are a strength.

A business architect, a process specialist and an application architect do not need to describe the same thing in the same way. A process and a capability may even have similar names without anything being wrong. They describe different aspects of the organisation.

The problem begins when we can no longer distinguish them or understand how they relate.

The goal is not for everyone to draw alike, or for every professional discussion to end in agreement. But independence cannot mean that central concepts change meaning between assignments, or that every agent invents its own object and relationship types without challenge.

We needed to distinguish two things.

The first was a shared ontology describing the kinds of objects and relationships we use.

The second was our own business knowledge: the actual processes, capabilities, information objects, applications and relationships in our organisation.

The ontology could provide a shared structure. Knowledge about our organisation still had to be built, reviewed and governed together.

The team therefore needed a shared foundation for:

  • the kinds of business objects and relationships we use;
  • reusing identified objects and relationships across analyses;
  • documenting sources, judgements and uncertainty; and
  • clarifying which questions each analysis should help the organisation answer.

Within that frame, working methods could still differ.

AI made the need for governance visible

The inconsistency was not new. Previously, it grew more slowly and hid inside documents that were rarely compared. AI made it larger and harder to ignore.

That was useful.

It also changed how we thought about quality assurance. It was not enough for each architect to review the output of their own agent. We had to determine whether the results could be used together.

If an agent identified an information object already described elsewhere, we needed to decide whether it really was the same object. If another suggested a relationship, we had to distinguish what was allowed by our shared structure from what was actually evidenced in the organisation.

And if two analyses contradicted each other, the difference had to remain visible rather than disappear when we selected the more attractive model.

This was not about controlling every detail. It was about enabling everyone’s work to contribute to the same whole.

My task was not to approve everything the agents produced. It was to create a way of working in which the team’s combined output could be understood, challenged and reused.

Individual productivity was welcome.

Shared reliability was the condition.
