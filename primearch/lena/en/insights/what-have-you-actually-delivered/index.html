What are we actually getting for our money?

Part 1 of 5

What have you actually delivered?

At a management meeting, I was asked a question I should have been better prepared for.

“Lena, what have we actually gained from the architecture work over the past six months?”

I had an answer.

We had documented business capabilities, updated process models, analysed application dependencies and supported several change initiatives. The team had also begun using AI and could prepare material faster than before.

After about a minute, I could see that I was losing the room.

There was nothing wrong with my account of the work.

The problem was the question I had chosen to answer.

Management was not asking how busy we had been

They wanted to know which risks we had made visible. Which investments had been supported by better evidence. Which changes could now be made with greater confidence, and which ones we had advised against.

I told them how much we had produced.

They asked what that production had made possible.

The distinction is uncomfortable, particularly when you lead a team that works hard.

Models, analyses and workshops are tangible. They can be counted. A decision that improved because someone identified a dependency in time is harder to capture.

But that is where the value lies.

I thought of a change initiative in which we had discovered that two applications handled the same business information differently.

That discovery affected the recommendation about which solution the organisation should choose.

We had contributed to a better basis for the decision.

Yet when I reported the team’s work, I counted it as another completed analysis.

I had described the deliverable, not what it had contributed to.

I had allowed the backlog to become our mission

When I reviewed the previous six months, I found many good pieces of work.

I also saw that we had accepted assignments without always clarifying which decision they were meant to support.

“Produce a capability map.”

“Document the information flows.”

“Create a current-state architecture.”

All of these can be reasonable activities.

None of them explains, on its own, why the work is needed or when it is good enough.

I had wanted to protect the team from unclear expectations. Instead, I had sometimes accepted those expectations and translated them into more work.

It is an easy trap for a leader to fall into.

You want to demonstrate that the team contributes. You want to be helpful. You do not want the architecture function to be perceived as an obstacle.

And suddenly you have a backlog full of tasks, but no convincing explanation of why those tasks matter most.

We started from the other end

At the next prioritisation meeting, nothing entered the backlog until we had answered three questions:

  • Which decision or change will this work support?
  • Who will use the result?
  • What will become possible to do — or avoid — if we succeed?

That did not mean every architecture initiative had to produce an immediate financial saving.

A shared ontology, a well-maintained business model or clear principles for describing information can create value over many years.

But such investments still need an intention.

They may make impact analysis easier, allow knowledge to be reused, reduce dependence on individuals or enable several architects to work with the same concepts and relationships.

It is not always possible to calculate the value in monetary terms on every occasion.

But we must be able to explain which capability we are building and how it will be used.

I did not stop monitoring what the team produced.

I stopped using production as my main answer to the question of value.

And I began to realise that our next challenge was not only about which assignments we accepted.

It was also about how we worked together.

If I cannot explain which decisions our architecture work helps the organisation make, I cannot expect management to make that connection for me.

And if our architects and AI agents do not work from a shared foundation, demonstrating the value of our combined work becomes harder still.
