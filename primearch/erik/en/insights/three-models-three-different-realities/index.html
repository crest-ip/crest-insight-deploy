When AI starts drawing the organisation

Part 2 of 5

Three models. Three different realities.

After my first attempt, I decided to run a more orderly experiment.

I gave the AI the same source material and asked it to produce three models: a process model, a capability model and an information model. I made sure that all three questions concerned the same business situation.

The result looked promising.

The process showed how a customer case was registered, assessed, handled and closed. The capability model showed what the organisation needed to be able to do in order to perform the work. The information model described the case, customer, decision and documentation.

Three intelligible pictures.

Then I tried to answer a simple question: what information does the organisation need in order to make a decision in the process?

I could not answer it without first reinterpreting the models.

The similarity was mostly in the words

In the process model, a decision was the outcome of an activity. In the information model, the AI had described a decision as a document. In the capability model, decision-making capability was used as the name of something the organisation needed to be able to do.

There is nothing inherently wrong with describing a decision from several perspectives. An activity may result in a decision, which may in turn be documented and used as information in subsequent work.

But a decision is not automatically the same thing as the document in which it is recorded. And the capability to make decisions is not the same thing as the decision-making activity itself.

Those were the distinctions the AI had failed to maintain.

I found the same pattern elsewhere. Customer case was both an information object and the name of a process. Assessment was an activity in one model and a capability in another. Account manager was a role, an organisational unit and sometimes merely a label on a handover.

The problem is not that the same word appears in several models. It begins when we can no longer tell what the word denotes in each context or how the different things relate.

AI did not invent this problem. People do the same thing. We draw a process in one project, an information model in another and a capability map before the next planning round. Each group uses the words that work in its own context.

The difference is speed.

AI could create three models before we had clarified the first central concept. It could also create three new variants as soon as I pointed out a contradiction.

I had more material to review, but not necessarily more knowledge.

I began looking for what they shared

My first response was to harmonise the models afterwards. I changed names, moved objects and added relationships. After a while, the pictures looked more consistent.

But I had mostly improved the surface.

What I lacked was a shared understanding of the kinds of things the models were allowed to describe. What is an activity? What is a capability? What is an information object? When are two labels synonyms, and when do they describe genuinely different things?

Above all: which relationships between them are meaningful?

An activity can use information. A capability may be required to perform work. A decision may be the outcome of an activity. But the mere fact that two objects appear in the same context does not make any relationship between them reasonable.

Without that foundation, every new model became a new negotiation.

This was where I had to remind myself of my own weakness. I enjoy untangling concepts. It is easy for me to turn a question that needs a useful answer by Friday into an extensive discussion of metamodels.

We did not need to define the entire organisation.

We needed to define enough for the three models to answer the same question without their meaning drifting.

Consistency is a relationship between models

I had long thought of model quality as something assessed one model at a time. Is the process readable? Are the information objects clearly named? Are the capabilities expressed at the right level?

Those remain important questions.

But the AI experiment brought another question into sharper focus: can the models be used together?

A process model can be excellent yet impossible to connect to the information model. A capability map can be well formulated yet use concepts that bear no relationship to the work. The problem only becomes visible when someone tries to follow a relationship across the boundary between models.

That is when a business question becomes a quality test.

If we change how customer cases are assessed, which activities are affected? Which information must change? Which capabilities need to be developed? Which systems use the same concept with a different meaning?

Three pictures are not enough to answer those questions.

We need to be able to follow the relationships between what the pictures describe.

That is an important distinction. The diagrams should not be what holds business knowledge together. That role belongs to the commonly defined objects and their relationships. Diagrams are different ways of showing parts of the same knowledge.

So I stopped asking the AI for the next model.

Instead, I began thinking about what it needed to know before it drew anything at all.
