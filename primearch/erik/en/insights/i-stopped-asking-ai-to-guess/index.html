When AI starts drawing the organisation

Part 3 of 5

I stopped asking AI to guess

At first, I tried to solve the inconsistency with better instructions.

I wrote longer prompts. I attached more examples. I asked the AI to be precise about the distinction between process, activity, capability and information.

The results improved.

But the improvement was fragile. If I changed the source material or phrased the question differently, the old shifts returned. The AI followed the instruction in the moment, but it still had no stable context to draw on next time.

I realised that I was still asking it to guess — only on a better-informed basis.

From glossary to shared foundation

I began by separating two things I had previously mixed together.

The first was the organisation’s own concepts: customer case, customer, assessment, decision and supporting material.

The second was the kinds of business objects those concepts represented and the relationships that made sense between them.

They are not the same thing.

An ontology does not need to know which customer cases exist in our organisation. But it does need to distinguish, for example, between an activity, a capability, a role and an information object.

And it needs to provide guidance on how they can relate.

So I did not begin by writing yet another glossary. I gave the AI a shared structure to work from.

An activity describes something that is performed. A capability describes what the organisation needs to be able to do. An information object describes information the organisation needs to handle. A role describes a responsibility or function in the work.

We then needed to describe the actual objects in our organisation.

Customer case could be an information object. Assess customer case could be an activity. Case management could be a capability.

But they did not become the same object merely because their names resembled one another.

Nor could relationships arise simply because they sounded plausible.

If an activity used information, we needed to show which information object was involved. If a role performed an activity, we needed to distinguish that from organisational accountability. If a capability was required to perform particular work, the relationship needed to be understandable and open to challenge.

This was not a complete mapping exercise.

It was a sufficiently clear foundation for the questions we actually wanted to answer.

The AI became less certain and more useful

I added the organisation’s source material to the structure.

But I did not want the AI to treat every sentence in the documents as established truth.

An interview note may describe how someone perceives the work. An approved instruction may describe how it is meant to be performed. A process model may represent an earlier attempt to establish a common view.

All may be relevant, but they do not necessarily say the same thing.

I therefore asked the AI to distinguish between what could be evidenced, what required interpretation and what remained unknown.

When I asked for the same three models again, the result was not merely more consistent.

The AI also began pointing out what could not be determined.

An activity had no clear accountability in the source material. Two documents used decision differently. A relationship between an assessment and a particular information object was possible but unsupported by evidence.

Previously, such gaps had often been filled with plausible assumptions.

Now they became visible.

That still required me to review the result. An agent can miss a contradiction or misinterpret a source even when it has clear rules.

But I had something better to review.

The models contained more questions and fewer confident conclusions.

I trusted them more for precisely that reason.

Structure is not the same as truth

It is tempting to believe that an ontology or a well-defined metamodel solves the quality problem.

It does not.

An ontology helps us distinguish between kinds of objects and understand which relationships are meaningful. It cannot determine whether our description of a particular organisation is actually correct.

I can still identify the wrong objects. I can connect things that have no real relationship. I can choose a level of detail that interests me but is useless to the person making the decision.

And, of course, I can ask the wrong questions.

I have made all those mistakes before, without AI.

Structure does not make the analysis correct automatically.

It makes it easier to discover and discuss where the analysis does not hold together.

That is an important distinction.

With a shared foundation, we can test whether two models describe the same business objects consistently. We can trace which sources support a claim. We can reuse knowledge without starting again from scratch.

But we still have to determine what we need to know and why.

That, to me, is where the real change in working practice lies.

The AI should not simply produce models for me.

It should help me investigate the organisation, ask better questions and build knowledge that can be used again.

The next test was change

While the models described the current state, making them hold together was relatively straightforward.

The real test came when I added a new requirement.

Suddenly, we did not merely need to describe the organisation. We needed to understand what would be affected if something changed.

That was when the shared structure would either prove its value or expose its limitations.
