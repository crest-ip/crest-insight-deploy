When AI starts drawing the organisation

Part 5 of 5

It is not the diagrams I want to keep

After a few weeks of experiments, I had accumulated a great many pictures.

Processes, capabilities, information, accountability, systems and chains of change. Some models were useful. Others were mainly interesting attempts. Several had already been replaced by better versions.

My first instinct was to organise them.

I created folders, naming conventions and a versioning structure. It felt reassuring. I am, after all, a business architect. Organising models is a reflex.

Then it occurred to me that I was preserving the wrong thing.

The diagram was only an answer for that moment

Most of the models had been created for a particular question. How is a customer case handled? Which capabilities are required? What is affected by a new review requirement?

When the question changed, the picture also needed to change.

That does not mean the diagrams lacked value. They helped us understand, discuss and test our reasoning. But their durability did not lie mainly in the layout or in the precise objects that happened to be visible.

The valuable parts were those that could be used again:

  • what central concepts meant;
  • which business objects had been identified;
  • how those objects related to one another;
  • which rules determined what kinds of relationship were meaningful;
  • which sources supported different claims;
  • where we had marked uncertainty; and
  • which questions remained open.

I also began to distinguish more clearly between two things.

The shared ontology described the kinds of business objects and relationships we could work with. It gave us a language and structure for describing organisations consistently.

Our own business model contained knowledge about our particular organisation: the identified objects, their actual relationships, the sources and the questions we had not yet answered.

That was the business knowledge I wanted to preserve, with the ontology as its shared foundation.

I began to think of it as structural capital.

Not as another large repository project, but as the part of our work that should outlive the individual deliverable.

The next question should not start from scratch

In many projects, we invest considerable effort in understanding the organisation. We interview, define, model and establish agreement. When the project ends, the result is archived. The next initiative begins with new interviews and a new discussion about what the same words mean.

AI can make that cycle faster.

That is not necessarily an improvement.

If every agent builds its own temporary picture, we can start again more often and at greater speed. We get more models, but no shared knowledge that grows from one question to the next.

The alternative is to let the agent work with a shared ontology and begin from the business knowledge we have already built.

A new analysis can then reuse established objects and relationships, while the agent helps us identify what needs to be added or reconsidered.

That naturally requires the knowledge to be accessible, traceable and governed. An ontology does not, by itself, resolve where our business information should be stored or how it should be kept current.

But it gives us a shared structure to work within, regardless of which tools or agents we use.

Reuse does not mean that old descriptions are always correct.

It means we can see what we already know, what we previously assumed and what needs to be investigated again.

I still need to know when to stop modelling

There is a risk here that I know well.

Once knowledge can be reused, making it complete becomes tempting. More concepts. More relationships. More rules for things that may become important later.

I have made that mistake before.

There is always one more relationship to investigate. One more information object to define. One more view that might prove useful.

But structural capital does not arise from describing everything.

It arises from preserving what will help us answer important questions better next time.

That requires boundaries.

Which decision are we trying to support? Which relationships do we need to understand? What uncertainty can we accept? When does further modelling cost more than it contributes?

I have become increasingly convinced that it is better to begin with the question than with the model.

A good structure does not merely help the AI reason more consistently.

It also helps me know when I have done enough.

From method to practical working approach

This is where I see a natural role for Prime Arch.

Not as a machine that automatically draws the correct organisation, and not as a definitive account of how our organisation works.

Prime Arch provides a shared ontology with defined types of business objects, relationships and modelling rules. It can give AI agents a stable point of departure when they help us describe, analyse and understand an organisation.

But the actual knowledge of our organisation must still come from us, our sources and our work.

That is an important division of responsibility.

The ontology helps us keep track of what we are describing and how different phenomena may relate.

The agent helps us investigate, structure, relate and explain.

We humans must still judge whether the description is accurate and useful for the question we are trying to answer.

What matters to me, therefore, is not the product name or the diagram on the screen.

What matters is whether we can move from a question to a line of reasoning and then see what that reasoning is based on.

Whether the next question can reuse that knowledge.

Whether someone else can discover when I, or the agent, have reached a conclusion too quickly.

And whether we can change our description without having to begin again from scratch.

AI will probably create more business models than we previously had time to produce.

The quantity will not determine whether the work improves.

What matters is whether we build knowledge that remains useful when the next change arrives.

It is not the diagrams I want to keep. It is the understanding behind them.
