When AI starts drawing the organisation

Part 4 of 5

What happens if we change this?

The stress test arrived in the form of a seemingly limited requirement.

The organisation wanted certain customer cases to receive a second assessment before a decision was made. The requirement applied to a specific type of case and had to be introduced without extending processing times more than necessary.

I asked the AI to analyse the impact.

Its first answer was quick and well formulated. The process needed an additional control step. The system needed to support a new status value. A role needed to become responsible for the second assessment.

It all sounded reasonable.

I could have put the answer into a presentation and moved on.

But this time, I wanted to know how the AI had reached its conclusions.

Following the change through the relationships

We began with the requirement and followed the relationships in the shared business model.

Which cases were in scope? Which activity performed the first assessment? What supporting material was used? When was the decision made? Which roles carried out the work, who was accountable for the decision, and which applications handled the information?

It was important to distinguish between what was actually described and what merely seemed plausible.

A relationship between an activity and an information object might show that the information was used in the work. But it did not automatically tell us how that information was stored, which application handled it or who was accountable for its quality.

Those conclusions required their own relationships and their own evidence.

It soon became clear that an additional control step was not the only possible consequence.

The second assessment needed to be distinguishable from the first. The decision needed to show which assessments it was based on. A customer notification currently used only the final status and contained no information about the review path followed by the case. Performance reporting also measured elapsed time from registration to decision without separating waiting time from active assessment.

The change therefore affected process, information, accountability and system support.

But not all those consequences had the same status.

Some could be derived from existing descriptions. Others were reasonable hypotheses requiring investigation. Still others were proposals for how the new requirement might be implemented.

I did not want the AI to conflate those three categories.

Then came the most important result

The AI also marked two relationships as uncertain.

The evidence showed which application handled the decision, but not whether individual assessments were stored as separate information objects. Nor was it clear whether the new reviewer would hold independent decision-making accountability or merely provide an opinion.

We could not therefore reach a reliable conclusion about the required system changes or the future distribution of accountability.

Previously, the AI had often filled such gaps with a plausible assumption.

Now they appeared as questions.

I did not regard that as a failure.

It was a better outcome than a complete list built on hidden assumptions.

An impact analysis does not have to provide every answer. It needs to show which conclusions are supported by evidence, which rely on interpretation and which require further knowledge.

There is a difference between observing that a change affects an information need and proposing exactly how an application should be rebuilt.

I wanted the AI to respect that difference.

I could still have asked the wrong question

At the same time, there was a danger of becoming too satisfied with the structure.

We had followed the change through the objects and relationships we already knew. That did not mean we had captured everything that mattered.

One caseworker pointed out, for example, that some cases arrived through a channel in which supporting information was often provided verbally. That variation was not visible in the models.

I could have built an elegant chain of impact and still missed how the work was actually performed.

It was a useful reminder.

A shared ontology helps us structure and relate knowledge. It does not guarantee that our knowledge of the organisation is complete.

AI can follow the relationships we have made available. It can also help identify possible gaps. But it cannot guarantee that we have chosen the right scope or understood every variation in the work.

We still need to speak to the people who actually do it.

And I must admit that it is sometimes easier to remain in front of a well-ordered model than to go out and discover that reality is more complicated.

Reliability is something we can investigate

After the experiment, I was less interested in whether the AI’s answer sounded convincing.

Instead, I looked for four things:

  1. Could I trace the business objects and relationships on which the conclusion was based?
  2. Could I see which sources supported the claims?
  3. Was it clear what was evidenced, interpreted or still unknown?
  4. Could someone in the organisation test the reasoning against their actual work?

When those questions could be answered, the AI became useful in a different way.

Not as a machine that delivered the definitive answer, but as a colleague who could help me investigate the change without concealing the path to the conclusion.

It was not only the result that had improved.

I now had a working practice in which the next question could build on what we already knew instead of beginning with a new set of assumptions.

And that was when I began to understand what I actually wanted to preserve from our work.
