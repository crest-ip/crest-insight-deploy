When AI starts drawing the organisation

Part 1 of 5

It actually looked rather good

I asked an AI to describe how a customer case moves through the organisation.

I gave it several documents, some interview notes and a simple description of the roles usually involved. A few minutes later, I had a proposed process in front of me.

It was tidy. The activities appeared in a sensible order. The handovers looked familiar. Even some of the exceptions we tend to discuss were included.

It actually looked rather good.

I will admit that I was impressed. Not because the model was finished, but because the AI had done in minutes what could easily have taken several workshops and multiple rounds of sticky notes.

Then I asked the next question.

I asked it to describe the business capabilities required to carry out the process.

That model looked reasonable too.

It was only when I put the two models side by side that something began to feel wrong.

The same word had acquired a different meaning

In the process model, case management was an end-to-end flow from registration to closure. In the capability model, the same term had become one capability among several.

That is not necessarily wrong. A process describes how work is carried out. A capability describes what the organisation needs to be able to do in order to carry it out. The same label may appear in both contexts, provided that we are clear about what the objects mean and how they relate.

But here, we were not clear.

The AI had not made the distinction understandable. Nor could it explain how the process and the capability were connected.

An activity in the process had also become a capability in the next model, with no explanation of why. An information object first called customer case had been renamed case documentation, as though the case itself and its documentation were the same thing. A role had turned into organisational accountability without any relationship showing why.

Each model worked on its own.

Together, they no longer quite described the same organisation.

I began checking the individual elements. Could the activity really be performed by the proposed role? Did it use the information the model claimed? Was the capability something the organisation needed to be able to do, or simply another name for the work already visible in the process?

I know I can be rather particular about this sort of thing. I have occasionally spent more time untangling a relationship than anyone else thought reasonable.

But this time, it was not about my fondness for detail.

The more questions I asked, the more small shifts I found.

None of them looked particularly dramatic. That was almost what concerned me most.

An obvious error is often easier

If the AI had placed invoicing before ordering, I would have spotted the error immediately. If it had invented a role that did not exist, someone in the organisation would have reacted.

But these models were plausible. They used the right sort of language. They followed familiar patterns. They looked like something an experienced modeller might have produced.

The problem was not that they were incomprehensible.

The problem was that their meaning changed from one model to the next.

That is a more troublesome kind of error because it can live on. One model becomes the input to another. A concept is copied into a requirement. A relationship becomes an assumption in an impact analysis. Before long, no one knows where the first shift occurred.

At first, I thought I simply needed a better prompt. More context. Clearer instructions. More examples.

That helped to a point.

But I also realised that I was trying to solve a structural problem with more text.

What did the AI actually have to hold on to?

I had asked the AI to create several models of the same organisation. I had not, however, given it a shared foundation for what the central objects meant, which relationships were valid or which sources should take precedence when the material contradicted itself.

The AI was doing roughly what I had asked: producing a convincing answer to each question.

I had not given it particularly good conditions for preserving the same concepts and relationships from one model to the next.

This does not mean that AI is unsuitable for business modelling. Quite the opposite. Its speed lets us test more hypotheses, explore alternative structures and make more progress before the next workshop.

But speed changes the quality problem too.

When a model takes a week to create, there is often time to notice which concepts need to be clarified. When five models appear in ten minutes, inconsistency can grow faster than the review process.

So I began with a simpler question than I had first intended:

Which parts of the organisation must mean the same thing in every model?

That became the beginning of a different way of working. Not one in which the AI did less, but one in which it had something clearer to hold on to.

Because creating a diagram that looks right is not especially difficult.

The difficult part is knowing whether the next diagram still describes the same reality.

I had found contradictions between two models. Then I asked the AI to describe the information used by the process.

That was when things became really interesting.
