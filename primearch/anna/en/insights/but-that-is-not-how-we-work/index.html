Everyone agreed. So why did it still go wrong?

Part 2 of 5

But that is not how we work!

“But that is not how we work!”

The words came from across the table, faster and sharper than I had expected.

We were reviewing an AI-generated proposal for an improved way of working. This time, I had made it clear that it was a hypothesis. Even so, I felt myself becoming defensive.

“No, not exactly today,” I said. “The idea is to see whether this could work better.”

The caseworker shook her head.

“That is not what I mean. We cannot perform that check there. We do not have the information yet.”

The lawyer turned back a page.

“And even if you had it, the grounds on which you use it must be clear.”

The IT service manager looked at the same box and said:

“That system cannot provide the information in that way.”

Three people. The same proposal. Three entirely different objections.

I wanted to pull the meeting together

One of my strengths as a change leader is that I can usually get people talking to one another. I hear when two people are saying almost the same thing in different words. I can often find wording everyone can gather around.

But that strength has a downside.

I want us to move on. When a meeting starts to scatter, I quickly look for common ground. This time I heard myself say:

“All right, then we agree that the check needs adjusting.”

Silence.

We did not agree. We merely had three different reasons why the same box did not work.

The caseworker was talking about when information arose in the work. The lawyer was talking about authority and legal basis. IT was talking about what the systems could actually exchange.

Had I summarised all of that as “adjust the check”, we would have lost the most important knowledge in the room.

The objections were not obstacles

In change work, it is easy to put reactions into two piles: those that move us forward and those that hold us back.

That is often a poor classification.

“That is not how we work” can certainly be a reflex. But it can also be the shortest possible version of several years’ experience. It may mean that an exception is more common than the rule, that a particular piece of information has no owner, or that an apparently unnecessary step protects something no one has documented.

This time, the objections were not resistance to change. They were business knowledge that had never made it into the material.

The AI had filled the gaps with assumptions that were individually plausible. A check needs information, so it assumed the information was available at the right time. A decision requires someone authorised to make it, but the AI proposed a role without checking the allocation of responsibility. The systems appeared in the documents, so it assumed they could exchange information in the way the process required.

None of it looked absurd.

That was exactly why we needed the people in the room.

We began collecting reasons, not merely corrections

I changed the review.

For each part of the proposal, we no longer simply marked it right or wrong. We tried to understand what the description rested on.

We asked five questions:

  • What do we know?
  • Who knows it?
  • Which source supports it?
  • What are we assuming?
  • What needs to be decided?

Suddenly, the conversation became less entrenched.

The caseworker did not have to defend today’s way of working. She could explain when the information actually became available.

The lawyer could distinguish between what the regulations required, what was not permitted, and what still had to be investigated or decided.

IT could show which limitations were technical and which were the result of earlier priorities.

And I began to understand that we were not really reviewing a single thing.

We were reviewing how the work was performed, what information it required, which rules applied and what system support existed.

They were different parts of the same organisation. They did not need to be described in exactly the same way.

But we did need to understand how they fitted together.

I therefore asked the AI to stop proposing new solutions for a while. Instead, it helped us compile what we actually knew, which statements contradicted one another and which questions needed answers.

That worked better.

Not because the AI suddenly understood the entire organisation, but because we had changed the task.

The AI proposal became worse as a finished solution and better as shared working material.

That is an improvement I now welcome.

A process diagram that everyone nods at can still conceal that people mean different things.

An objection allowed to remain visible for long enough can show us what we genuinely need to understand.

The next time I opened the AI, I did not begin by asking for another proposal.

I began with the questions.
