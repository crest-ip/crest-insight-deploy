Everyone agreed. So why did it still go wrong?

Part 5 of 5

It was not the process diagram that changed anything

Eventually, we had a process diagram everyone could support.

It was not revolutionary. It showed how a case arrived, which assessments were required, which roles performed the work and how the individual received feedback. The exceptions were included. The information needs were clearer. We could also show which parts of the work were supported by different applications.

A few months earlier, I would probably have regarded the diagram as the deliverable.

Now I knew better.

What changed the work was not the boxes and arrows.

It was everything we had needed to do before we could draw them without pretending.

We had learnt where we meant different things

The caseworker and the lawyer had clarified what a complete set of information meant at different stages. IT had shown which data could genuinely be retrieved and which required changes.

The responsible managers had made decisions about matters previously allowed to fall between the cracks.

We had also documented our uncertainties.

That may sound minor, but it made a considerable difference.

When someone asked why a step looked the way it did, we could trace the reasoning back to a source, a judgement or a decision. When conditions changed, we knew which parts needed to be reviewed.

We had also begun separating things that had previously been mixed together.

An activity was something performed. A role described who performed the work. An information object described information that was required or created. Accountability for a decision did not have to rest with the same person who performed every activity leading up to it.

I had not previously thought those distinctions particularly remarkable.

But once we described them consistently, it became much easier to understand why we sometimes talked past one another.

The process diagram became useful because it did not stand alone.

AI improved when we stopped demanding that it know

At first, I used AI to get an answer quickly.

By the end, we used it for several smaller tasks.

It compared how different documents used the same term. It highlighted steps with no clear accountability. It suggested possible consequences when an information need changed. It helped us summarise open questions before meetings and investigate whether a new proposal conflicted with something we had already decided.

Sometimes it was wrong.

The difference was that we were better equipped to detect the errors.

We had begun working with shared definitions, visible sources and clearer relationships between parts of the organisation.

We had also given the AI a shared structure for the kinds of business objects it should distinguish and how they could relate.

That did not make the AI infallible.

But it made its proposals easier to review and made it easier to ask follow-up questions when something looked wrong.

The AI did not have to play the part of something that understood the entire organisation.

It could help us work with the understanding we had built together.

Consensus is not the same as accountability

I have also become more careful with the word agreement.

A room full of nodding people does not necessarily mean everyone has understood the same thing.

And shared understanding does not automatically make change happen.

Someone must still decide. Someone must change a way of working, prioritise a system change, update an instruction and follow up what happens in everyday practice.

My task is to help that chain hold together, not to become personally accountable for every link.

That was an important lesson for me.

For a long time, I thought my job was to bring everyone with me.

Now I more often think my job is to help people understand what they need to do together, which decisions must be made and who is actually accountable for what.

It may sound like a small distinction.

For me, it has been quite a large one.

A foundation from which to keep changing

This is where I see the value of a shared ontology such as Prime Arch.

Not as a finished description of our organisation, and not as a place where all our knowledge is automatically gathered.

But as a shared foundation that helps people and AI agents distinguish between activities, information, roles, capabilities and applications — and understand which kinds of relationships between them are meaningful.

We still have to build, establish and maintain our own business knowledge.

The ontology does not do that for us.

But it can help us describe the knowledge in a way that remains consistent when we ask new questions.

And there will be a next time.

A new regulation. A new political objective. A system to replace. A service that must work better for the people we are here to serve.

Then I do not want to begin by asking an AI what the perfect process looks like.

I want to begin by asking:

What do we know? What do we mean? What is missing? Who needs to be involved? And who owns the decision?

The process diagram can help us understand what we need to do.

But shared understanding, explicit decisions and people who take responsibility are what make change happen.
