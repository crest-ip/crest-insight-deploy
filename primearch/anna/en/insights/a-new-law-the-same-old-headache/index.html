Everyone agreed. So why did it still go wrong?

Part 4 of 5

A new law. The same old headache.

Imagine a new regulatory requirement arriving on a Monday morning.

It will take effect in a few months. At first, the wording looks quite limited: the individual must receive clearer information about how a particular decision was made and which data was used.

Then the questions begin.

Which decisions are affected? Where is the data? Which texts need changing? Must the systems display something new? Who owns the assessment? And which ongoing initiatives are affected?

I have been through enough changes to recognise the pattern.

The new requirement is rarely the only headache.

The old headache is that no one can easily show everything the requirement touches.

I used to start running

I would probably have called a large meeting, created a list and begun chasing answers.

Legal would interpret the requirement. Operations would look for affected working practices. IT would inventory the systems. Communications would review the texts.

I would try to hold it all together and, if I am honest, take responsibility for the gaps between the groups.

It is a role I know a little too well.

When no one quite knows who owns a question, the business developer easily becomes the person who tries to solve it.

This time, I would begin differently.

I would give the AI the requirement itself and the business knowledge we had already assembled. Then I would ask for an initial view of possible consequences:

  • which ways of working and decisions might be affected;
  • which information is used in those contexts;
  • which roles perform the work and where accountability needs clarification;
  • which applications handle the information;
  • which sources support the identified relationships; and
  • where the evidence is insufficient for a conclusion.

The last point is crucial.

A list without visible uncertainty can easily look more settled than it is.

AI may propose. People must judge.

AI can identify that a term in the regulation resembles one used in our process descriptions. It can find information appearing in several contexts and suggest which parts of the organisation may be affected.

But similar words do not automatically mean the same thing.

And a relationship that seems plausible is not necessarily one that exists in our organisation.

That is why I want the AI to distinguish between what we have already described, what the sources actually support and what it proposes we investigate.

It may help us find possible consequences.

But it does not decide what the law requires. It does not decide whether a particular use of data is lawful. And it does not choose which change the organisation should make.

That work must still be owned by the right people.

The lawyer assesses the legal interpretation. Operational owners assess how working practices are affected. Information and system owners investigate consequences in their areas. Authorised decision-makers set priorities and take responsibility for the choices.

My role is not to carry all their decisions.

My role is to help them see the same question and understand how their parts fit together.

The same map, different responsibilities

What used to become several separate inventories can instead become a shared impact view.

But that shared view requires more than gathering all the information in one document.

We need to distinguish between different kinds of business objects and understand how they relate.

An activity is not the same as a capability. An information object is not the same as the application handling the information. A role that performs an activity need not hold formal accountability for the decision.

That may sound obvious.

Yet these are precisely the distinctions that disappear when we are in a hurry.

A shared ontology such as Prime Arch can help an AI work with clearer concepts and defined relationships.

But the ontology does not automatically contain our organisation’s actual processes, allocation of responsibility, systems or legal requirements.

We must supply that knowledge from our own sources and keep it current.

With that division of responsibility, AI supports the investigation of consequences rather than replacing the organisation’s judgements.

A lawyer can mark which interpretations are settled and which require investigation. A caseworker can test whether the description matches everyday reality. IT can determine which information flows and applications are genuinely affected.

And when someone finds an error, we can return to the evidence and see which conclusions need reconsideration.

It is still work.

There will still be discussions.

But at least we do not begin with five separate lists that no one knows how to connect.

And perhaps more importantly: I no longer need to be the human link expected to remember every dependency myself.

A new requirement will always create new questions.

It need not mean rediscovering the organisation every time.
