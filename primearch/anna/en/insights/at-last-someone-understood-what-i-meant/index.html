Everyone agreed. So why did it still go wrong?

Part 1 of 5

At last, someone understood what I meant

I had tried to explain the same problem three times.

It concerned a case that began simply enough but could pass through a caseworker, a lawyer and IT before anyone could give a final answer. Information arrived in several ways. Some checks were always performed, others only occasionally. If anything was missing, a small hunt began for the right person, the right document and the right version.

I had interview notes, a few old process diagrams and a folder of instructions that did not quite agree. It was the kind of situation in which everyone knew their part, but no one had time to describe the whole.

So I gave the material to an AI and asked it to propose a coherent way of working.

The answer arrived almost immediately.

It was clear. Incoming information was registered, checked and completed. Responsibility moved between the roles in a sensible way. Exceptions had their own paths. At the end came a decision, feedback and closure.

I remember thinking: At last, someone understood what I meant.

It looked like a real answer

I did what I often do when a question gives me energy. I began improving it.

I asked the AI to shorten lead times, remove unnecessary handovers and suggest what could be automated. It moved one check earlier in the flow, combined two activities and proposed a common entry point for information.

It was not science fiction. It looked feasible.

At our next review, I showed the proposal to an experienced caseworker. She read in silence for a moment, then pointed to a box.

“Who does this?” she asked.

I answered with the role shown in the proposal.

“But we are not authorised to make that decision.”

She pointed to the next step.

“And this information is almost never available when the case arrives.”

Then came the question that stopped me:

“Is this supposed to describe how we work, or how someone thinks we ought to work?”

I did not have a good answer.

The AI had not misunderstood my instruction

My first impulse was to defend the proposal. It was only a draft. Of course we would quality-assure it. No one had said it was finished.

But the caseworker was not criticising the colours or the order of the boxes. She was showing me that knowledge I had not supplied was missing.

The AI had no evidence showing which role held formal authority. It did not know that a document which, according to the instruction, should be present often arrived several days later. It had not been told that two units used the same term for different things, or that informal practices had developed to handle recurring problems.

Where the material contained gaps, the AI filled them with something plausible.

And I had not asked it to show where it was making assumptions.

This was not an incomprehensible answer. It was a convincing answer built on incomplete knowledge.

That is an important distinction.

I had confused recognition with understanding

I recognised the problem in the text and therefore assumed that the AI understood the organisation. What I recognised was mainly my own description, expressed more clearly.

It was tempting to see the speed as a breakthrough. In minutes, we had produced something that would otherwise have required several meetings.

In one sense, that was true.

The AI had given us an unusually good basis for discussion.

But I had treated it as an improvement proposal before we had tested whether its description of the current situation was accurate.

That was my mistake, not resistance from the caseworker.

I had also skipped a question I am usually careful about: what do we know, what do we believe, and what must we ask the people who actually do the work?

I think the speed deceived me.

When something takes weeks to develop, uncertainties often reveal themselves along the way. When a finished-looking proposal appears in minutes, it is easy to start discussing the solution before understanding the problem.

After the meeting, I changed the document’s heading. Instead of “New way of working”, I wrote:

This is what we think we know. What have we missed?

That did not make the proposal worse. It made our use of it more honest.

AI can be remarkably good at making a messy story visible very quickly. But a visible story is not the same as a shared understanding of the organisation.

I only learnt that when someone who actually did the work asked who was supposed to do it.

And I suspected there were more such questions we had yet to ask.
