Everyone agreed. So why did it still go wrong?

Part 3 of 5

I started asking different questions

I used to begin roughly like this:

“Propose a more efficient process for…”

It is a tempting question. It often produces a well-structured answer with clear steps, responsibilities and improvements.

The problem is that the word efficient sends the AI running before we humans have decided where we are going.

After a few less successful attempts, I changed my approach.

I no longer began by asking for a solution.

I began by asking the AI to help me see what we did not know.

Four questions changed the conversation

I gave the AI the same interview notes, instructions and process diagrams as before. But the task was different:

  1. What appears to be supported by the material?
  2. What is uncertain or contradictory?
  3. Which perspectives are missing?
  4. Which words seem to be used with different meanings?

The answer was less impressive to present to a steering committee. It contained no finished future state and no colourful arrows.

But it gave us better questions for the next workshop.

One word we had all used was complete.

For the caseworker, it meant that enough information was available to begin work. For the lawyer, it meant that the evidence was sufficient for a decision. For IT, it meant that every mandatory field had been completed.

We had used the same word in several meetings and nodded to one another.

We had not meant the same thing.

I had to slow my own desire for consensus

My habit is to find shared wording quickly. Here, I needed to do the opposite.

I had to let the differences remain visible for a while.

So we did not write a single definition of complete. We described three different situations:

  • when a case contains enough information for processing to begin;
  • when the evidence is sufficient for a decision to be made; and
  • when the mandatory information has been entered so the case can be registered in the system.

That may sound pedantic.

It was not.

The distinction determined when a case could move forward, who needed to act and which signals the system should provide.

We also discovered that three clearer definitions were not enough. We had to understand what they referred to.

They concerned the same case, but different information requirements at different stages of the work. Those requirements related to the activities that used the information and the decisions to be made.

As we described those relationships, several old conflicts became less personal.

No one had necessarily been careless. Two people had assessed the same case against different criteria.

The AI did not resolve that question for us.

It helped us find where we needed to talk.

From answer machine to investigation partner

This changed how I use AI in business development.

I still ask for proposals. But first, I want to know which assumptions support them.

I want to see which sources support a conclusion and which roles have yet to describe their day-to-day reality.

I want to distinguish between what we know, what we believe and what someone must actually decide.

Sometimes I ask the AI to formulate two competing interpretations of the same material. Sometimes it looks for a step with no clear owner. Sometimes it compares how a central concept is used in different documents.

It does not always make the meeting faster.

It more often makes the meeting honest.

And people become more engaged when we do not begin by presenting a solution they are expected to approve. It is easier to contribute when you are invited to help clarify the question.

A shared foundation need not be remarkable

We began gathering the most important descriptions of the work, information, roles and relationships between them.

Not to build a perfect answer, but to avoid starting from nothing each time we asked a new question.

At the same time, I discovered a difference between collecting our own knowledge and having a shared structure for describing it.

Our interviews and instructions said something about our particular organisation.

But for AI to help us consistently, it also needed to understand the difference between an activity, a role, an information object and a responsibility — and which kinds of relationships between them were meaningful.

This is where something such as Prime Arch can become practical.

Not as a ready-made description of our organisation, and not as a place where all our information is automatically gathered.

But as a shared ontology that helps AI agents distinguish between kinds of business objects and work with defined relationships.

Our own knowledge and sources must still provide the content.

For me, the point is simple:

AI does not only need more material. It needs a clearer foundation for how that material should be understood, what remains uncertain and which questions must be put to the organisation.

When I began working this way, I did not always receive a finished answer as quickly.

I received something more useful: a better way into the conversation.

And that would soon prove much more important than I had first thought.
