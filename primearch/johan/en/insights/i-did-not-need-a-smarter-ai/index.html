Three weeks to delivery

Part 3 of 5

I did not need a smarter AI

My first thought was predictable: I needed a better AI model. More capacity. A larger context window. Smarter reasoning.

What I mainly needed was to give the agent better working conditions.

I had supplied a pile of documents and asked for a coherent analysis. The agent had done roughly what I asked: it created coherence. The problem was that it had no clear boundary between what it could evidence, what it offered as a reasonable interpretation and what it simply did not know.

So I changed the assignment.

Four requirements that made a difference

First, I gave the agent a shared foundation for the kinds of business objects it was working with.

A process was not an application. An information object was not a database. The role performing an activity was not necessarily the role formally accountable for it.

Second, I used defined relationship types.

An activity using information does not mean a particular application automatically stores it. Two applications handling the same information does not mean they have a direct integration.

Third, I required traceability.

Every important conclusion had to connect to a source, a documented observation or an explicitly stated professional judgement.

Fourth, I made knowledge gaps visible.

I gave the agent a simple instruction:

Do not fill knowledge gaps with assumptions that look like facts. Show what is missing.

That last requirement was almost the hardest for me. A deliverable with visible gaps feels less finished. But a hidden gap does not become less real because it has been given polished wording.

Structure that earns its keep

I am sceptical of methodology that grows until no one remembers which client problem we meant to solve.

I did not want to spend two weeks constructing a perfect language for a three-week assignment. Nor did I need to.

Established ways of structuring business knowledge already exist. Prime Arch became useful to me as a shared ontology with defined business object and relationship types from which the agent could work.

That did not mean Prime Arch contained knowledge about the client’s organisation. We still had to obtain that from documents, interviews, existing models and people who knew the business.

But the shared ontology allowed us to describe that knowledge more consistently. That distinction mattered.

We limited the work to the decision the client needed to make. Which parts of the business would the system replacement affect? What information was used and exchanged? Which applications supported the work? Which dependencies were evidenced? Which assumptions could alter the recommendation?

We did not need to map the entire organisation. We needed enough knowledge to answer those questions.

We did not lose the speed

Quite the opposite.

With a shared structure, the agent did not have to improvise object and relationship types for each new analysis. Results became more consistent. Marking gaps immediately reduced the time I spent uncovering hidden assumptions later. Reusing identified business objects across views avoided separate copies that then had to be kept in sync.

This still required us to govern our business knowledge and make it available to the agent. An ontology does not do that work automatically. It provides a shared language and rules to work by.

I retained many of the productivity gains that had attracted me in the first place. The difference was that I could use them with greater professional confidence.

There is a temptation to treat structure and speed as opposites. Sometimes they are. If the structure costs more work than the question deserves, we have failed.

But the right amount of structure is not administration. It enables us to work quickly without every answer beginning with a new set of definitions and assumptions.

I did not need an AI that sounded more certain.

I needed a way of working in which both the agent and I could show what we knew, how we knew it and what we still only believed.
