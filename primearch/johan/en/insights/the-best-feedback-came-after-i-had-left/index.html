Three weeks to delivery

Part 5 of 5

The best feedback came after I had left

A former client called a few months after the assignment had ended.

I prepared myself for the usual question: Where is the latest version? What did you mean by this relationship? Can you help us interpret the model?

That was not why they called.

They had received a new requirement. The team had analysed which parts of the organisation might be affected, reused the objects and relationships we had documented and added new information.

They had also corrected two of my previous assumptions.

“We just wanted to check that you agreed with how we continued,” the project manager said.

I did. More importantly, I was impressed that they did not really need to ask.

A dependency that was allowed to end

Consulting almost always creates a dependency during an assignment. The client buys time, experience and capability that are not available internally at that moment.

The question is what happens afterwards.

If the client still needs the consultant to understand the deliverable, the knowledge has not been properly transferred. If an AI agent produced the analysis but nobody can explain its sources and assumptions, we have merely exchanged dependence on a person for dependence on technology.

In this case, the dependency could end.

The team had received more than a report. They had a business model containing identified objects, relationships, sources and documented judgements. They could see what was verified, what rested on assumptions and which questions remained open.

They also had a shared ontology from which to continue.

Prime Arch was part of that way of working, but not the point in itself. The ontology helped them describe the organisation consistently. It did not automatically contain knowledge about their organisation.

We had built that knowledge together. They could now govern it themselves.

That was exactly as it should be.

My speed still mattered

I had not changed my mind about pace. The client needed a decision in three weeks, not a multi-year mapping exercise.

AI helped me find my bearings faster, test more hypotheses and spend more time on the questions that actually affected the decision.

But speed is valuable only when it moves us towards a result that lasts.

I used to think of reusability as an extra: worthwhile if the budget allowed, but not central to the assignment. I now see it differently.

Reusability does not mean documenting everything. It means that the most important things can be understood and used without their creator sitting beside them.

Nor does it require the client to use the same tool or AI agent I used. What matters is that they have access to their own business knowledge and can continue working within a shared structure.

Then the next question can build on what we have already learnt.

The professional test

AI enables me to produce more than before. That is no longer particularly impressive in itself.

The professional test is harder:

Can I show where a conclusion came from? Can the client distinguish facts from assumptions? Can the result be corrected when reality proves different? Can someone else continue the work?

And perhaps most importantly: have I left something that genuinely helps the client make the next decision?

If the answer is yes, AI has done more than make me faster. It has helped me create more value in the same limited time.

The best feedback on a consulting assignment may therefore not be that the client calls back to ask for more help.

It may be that they call to tell you they managed the next step themselves.
