Three weeks to delivery

Part 2 of 5

That relationship — where did it come from?

“That relationship between the systems. Where did it come from?”

The question took five seconds to ask. My answer took considerably longer.

I knew why the relationship appeared in the model. One document said that data was registered in one system. An interview suggested that the same data was used in the other. The agent had concluded that an integration existed.

It was plausible. It was also wrong.

After a while, a system manager explained that the information was moved manually. Someone exported a file, corrected it and uploaded it again.

There was an exchange of information between parts of the organisation, but no direct system integration of the kind the diagram implied.

That was precisely the problem. I had allowed an assumption about how information moved to become a claim about how two applications were connected.

It was not a minor detail. It affected both the cost estimate and the risk assessment for the system replacement.

I had an argument, but no evidence

I could have defended the model. I could have said the relationship was conceptual, that the diagram needed to be read at the correct level of abstraction and that the details would be verified later.

Consultants can be surprisingly creative when an attractive diagram needs to survive a meeting.

But the client had not asked a methodological question. She wanted to know what the claim was based on.

I could not show her.

The agent had combined several clues. I had judged the result likely. Somewhere along the way, “likely” had become a line without a question mark.

There were really two errors. We had confused an information exchange with a technical integration, and we had failed to mark the relationship as a hypothesis.

The second concerned me almost more. Even if the model had happened to be correct, I would still have lacked proper evidence for the claim.

My name was on the deliverable

The AI was not sitting in the steering committee. It had no contract with the client. It did not have to explain how the conclusion arose or what an error meant for the decision.

I did.

That is why verifiability is more than a technical quality. For a consultant, it is part of trust.

The client must be able to distinguish between:

  • something directly supported by evidence;
  • an interpretation I stand behind professionally;
  • a hypothesis that needs testing; and
  • a gap where we do not yet know.

I also need to distinguish different kinds of relationship. A business activity using information says something about the work. An application handling information says something about system support. Two applications exchanging data is a different claim.

They may be related, but one does not automatically prove another.

If everything looks the same in the deliverable, the most confident wording will win. That is rarely a sound quality principle.

How much had to be redone?

After the meeting, I reviewed the model relationship by relationship.

The work took time, but the real problem was not the volume. It was that I lacked a consistent way to trace each claim back to its source.

Some could be verified directly. Others rested on interviews. Some were my own conclusions. Several had been proposed by the agent without making it clear that the sources were insufficient.

I also had to check that each relationship described the right kind of connection. A line in a diagram may look harmless, but the wrong relationship type can change the meaning of an entire analysis.

I had not saved two weeks. I had moved some of the work until after the presentation, when uncertainty was more expensive.

It was uncomfortable, but useful.

I stopped asking how to make the agent produce even better diagrams. I started asking what each object and relationship actually rested on — and whether the agent could work from a shared structure rather than inventing the connections afresh every time.
