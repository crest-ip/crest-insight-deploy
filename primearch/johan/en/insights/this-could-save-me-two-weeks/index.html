Three weeks to delivery

Part 1 of 5

This could save me two weeks

I had three weeks.

The client wanted a current-state view, an analysis of the information flows and a basis for deciding on a system replacement. The documentation was scattered across old project folders. The interviews were booked, but several key people were not available until the following week.

A fairly normal consulting assignment, in other words: high expectations and source material likely to become complete at roughly the same time as the final report.

This time, I had AI.

I gave the agent access to process diagrams, system lists, meeting notes and governance documents. It summarised the current state, proposed information objects and identified possible relationships between processes, roles and systems.

Two days later, I had material that would previously have taken at least two weeks to produce.

It looked very good.

Production was no longer the bottleneck

I could spend less time moving text between documents and more time thinking. The agent found contradictions I had missed, grouped questions for the interviews and prepared a first draft of the steering committee’s decision material.

It was exactly the kind of leverage I had hoped for.

Then I made a classic consulting mistake.

I allowed the finished appearance to influence my judgement of how finished the content was. The headings were in place. The diagrams looked coherent. The conclusions sounded reasonable.

When something looks like a deliverable, you start treating it like one.

I should have treated it as a hypothesis produced at remarkable speed.

Experience can fill gaps rather too effectively

I have worked in many similar organisations. That helps me find my bearings quickly.

When one document mentioned a customer register and another described case management, some connections felt obvious. A customer case needs customer information. That information must exist somewhere. Some system must handle it.

So far, the reasoning was sound.

But it is a sizeable leap from there to claiming that a particular application supplies a particular information object to another application. That relationship needs evidence.

The agent had done something I sometimes do myself when time is short: recognised familiar patterns and filled the gaps with what appeared likely. I recognised the overall picture, so I did not challenge every relationship as rigorously as I should have.

It was not only the AI that took a shortcut. I did too.

What had I actually saved?

AI had saved production time. It had not automatically saved verification time, and it certainly had not assumed my professional responsibility.

For every conclusion, I still needed to know:

  • which source supported it;
  • what was observation and what was interpretation;
  • which gaps had been filled with assumptions; and
  • who at the client could confirm or correct the picture.

I also needed to distinguish different kinds of relationship. A process using information does not automatically mean a particular application stores it. Two systems appearing in the same business flow does not mean they communicate directly.

These may sound obvious. Yet when an agent produces twenty plausible relationships in minutes, it is surprisingly easy to start treating them as established facts.

That did not make the AI work less valuable. Quite the opposite.

But the value was not that two weeks of work had vanished. It was that I could move time from production to challenge, dialogue and decision-making — provided I actually used the time that way.

On my way to the steering committee meeting, I was still rather pleased. I had a clear story, a concrete proposal and far better material than the timetable should have allowed.

Then an executive pointed to a line between two systems and asked a very simple question.
