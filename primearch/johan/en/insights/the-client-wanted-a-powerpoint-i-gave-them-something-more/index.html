Three weeks to delivery

Part 4 of 5

The client wanted a PowerPoint. I gave them something more.

The client had ordered a PowerPoint.

There is nothing wrong with that. A steering committee rarely needs a lecture on information models. It needs to understand the options, the consequences and the decision expected of it.

I delivered ten slides.

But this time, the slides were not the analysis. They were a way of presenting it.

Every conclusion had something behind it

When the presentation showed that an option created a critical dependency, we could return to the business model and examine the processes, information objects and applications involved.

We could see which relationships were documented and which remained assumptions. We could also trace the sources supporting the conclusions.

That was a significant change from previous deliveries.

In the past, I often had to explain what a line or box in the presentation actually represented. Sometimes I could refer to an interview, sometimes to a document and sometimes, if I am honest, to my own experience.

Now the distinction was already visible in the underlying material.

When the steering committee wanted to test another scenario, we did not have to start again. We could change one condition and use the existing objects and relationships to investigate possible consequences.

The model did not automatically give us the right answer. New assumptions still required review, and some consequences required more information from the business. But we had somewhere to continue from.

This was not a complete model of the organisation. That would have wasted both time and the client’s money. We had built just enough for the decision at hand, but in a form that could be used again.

The deliverable had two audiences

The first was the steering committee. They received the bounded decision material they had ordered.

The second was the team continuing after the decision. They received access to the business model: the identified objects, their relationships, sources, judgements and open questions.

They could distinguish what was verified, what had been decided and what was still proposed. They also knew which parts of the analysis needed governing as the organisation changed.

It is easy to forget the second audience.

Consulting assignments often reward the handover itself: the meeting went well, the report was accepted and the invoice could be sent.

But if the next team must interview the same people and interpret the same documents again, the deliverable has created less lasting value than we like to think. If all the knowledge lives in the presentation, it is difficult to know what to change when circumstances change.

I also gave up control

A reusable deliverable means the client can change it without me.

That sounds obvious. It can still feel uncomfortable for a consultant.

My experience and ability to understand the whole quickly are part of what the client buys. When that whole exists only in my head, I am difficult to replace. It is also a poor handover format.

This time, we tried to do better.

We used Prime Arch as a shared ontology for describing business objects and relationships consistently. The actual knowledge of the client’s organisation lived in the business model we built from the client’s material.

That model, together with sources, judgements and open questions, was what the client needed to take over. The ontology supplied a shared structure; it did not replace the client’s own knowledge or the need to govern it.

The client did not need to continue using my particular agent either. Another agent, with access to the same business knowledge and model rules, could help the team continue. Professional review would still be needed, but they would not start from a blank page.

The PowerPoint did its job. It helped the steering committee make a decision.

What pleased me most was barely visible in the room:

The client did not need to retain me to understand why the slides looked the way they did.
